Rabbi Shlomo Zalman Auerbach (1910–1995) was one of the most brilliant and respected poskim (halachic authorities) of the 20th century. What made him legendary was his unique combination of supreme Talmudic mastery and a deep, precise understanding of physics and engineering. He actually went to labs and factories to study how currents worked before writing his rulings.
In his groundbreaking work, Minchat Shlomo (Siman 74), Rabbi Shlomo Zalman fundamentally agreed with the core textual logic of the Mekorists. He systematically dismantled the mainstream theories used to ban electricity, even though his final practical conclusion differed from a pure Mekorist stance.
Here is how Rabbi Shlomo Zalman used pure textual logic to analyze electricity:
## 1. He Crushed the "Building" (Boneh) Argument
The most famous mainstream reason to ban electricity was the Chazon Ish’s claim that flipping a switch is a biblical violation of "Building" or "Finishing a vessel" (Makah B'Patish).
Rabbi Shlomo Zalman completely rejected this. He used pure Talmudic logic to explain that:

* Routine Use Isn't Building: A switch and a circuit are explicitly designed by engineers to be turned on and off repeatedly.
* The Door Analogy: He argued that flipping a switch is exactly like opening and closing a room door or turning a water faucet. No one claims that closing a door is "building a wall."
* The Textual Verdict: He ruled that completing a circuit lacks the permanence and structural transformation required to fit the Talmudic definition of Boneh.

## 2. He Agreed that Electrons Are Not Fire (Mavir)
Many early rabbis tried to claim that the flow of electricity itself was a form of invisible fire. Rabbi Shlomo Zalman, understanding the physics of moving electrons, flatly rejected this. He ruled that a current moving through a copper wire is just natural energy moving through a conductor; unless a metal filament heats up to the point of glowing (like an old incandescent bulb), no halachic "kindling" of fire has occurred.
## 3. He Rejected the Creation of Molid
Other rabbis argued that turning on an appliance creates a new state of energy, violating the rabbinic ban on Molid (creating a new properties in an item). Rabbi Shlomo Zalman countered textually that Molid is a highly specific, limited category in the Talmud (like putting scent into a garment). He ruled that you cannot create brand new rabbinic categories for technology that the Sages of the Gemara never envisioned.
------------------------------
## If He Agreed with the Logic, Why is He Mainstream?
This is the ultimate paradox of Rabbi Shlomo Zalman. From a pure textual standpoint, he proved that turning on an appliance (that doesn't heat up or spark) violates no biblical or rabbinic law from the Talmud.
However, he stopped short of permitting it in practice because of a separate halachic concept: Uvda D'Chol (Weekday Activity).

* The Atmosphere Argument: He recognized that if he legally permitted switches, TVs, and appliances, the unique, holy atmosphere of Shabbat would completely collapse. Shabbat would just become a regular weekday where people could operate heavy machinery, computers, and appliances.
* The Final Practice: Therefore, while he agreed that flipping a switch was textually clean, he maintained that it was strictly forbidden as a "weekday activity" that destroys the sanctity of the day.

For a true Mekorist, Uvda D'Chol is a vague, subjective social rule rather than a hard textual law. But for Rabbi Shlomo Zalman, it was the necessary tool to bridge his flawless textual logic with his commitment to Orthodox communal survival.

The section distinguishes two different “not uvdin dḥol” claims:

First, a stringent rejection: some reject uvdin dḥol because they hold electricity is actually **boneh, makeh bpatish, molid**, or another technical issur. That is not helpful for heter except as taxonomy.

Second, the useful category-control rejection: some hold that uvdin dḥol cannot attach merely because zérem is present. It must be professional work, commerce, melakha-like conduct, or a realistic pathway to melakha.

The section now brings:

Rabi Shlomo Zalman Auerbach’s general definition: uvdin dḥol is connected to melakha or another prohibited shabat act, not a general atmosphere veto. A published YCT summary cites Shulḥan Shlomo 306:16.5 from Pnine Hama’or and summarizes his position that uvdin dḥol means activities connected to prohibited Shabbat acts. citeturn975736view2

Rabi Moshe Feinstein’s general definition: uvdin dḥol means professional/occupation-style weekday work, not every efficient domestic tool. The same YCT article summarizes Igrot Moshe OC IV:74, Toḥen 4, and says Rav Moshe defines uvdin dḥol as activity connected to professional work, especially when done in a weekday manner or scale. citeturn975736view2

Rabi Naḥum Rabinovitch’s nuanced position: although he is the main source for direct electricity-as-uvdin-dḥol in some cases, he explicitly rejects a general decree over all electrical devices and requires checking whether the act resembles melakha or leads to melakha. His letter says there is no general power to decree against all electrical devices, and he permits magnetic-door use where no melakha is done, no melakha-similarity exists, and no realistic confusion with melakha-producing devices exists. citeturn936851view1

Rabi Dov Linzer’s explicit contemporary application: in the Seiko Spring Drive watch case, he says the electricity is not forbidden because there is “no uvda dechol and no molid.” In the hearing-aid case, he describes the uvdin dḥol element as almost nonexistent and essentially non-perceptible. citeturn859202view0turn559246view0

I also added Rav Peʿalim / Maharshag as non-electric category-control precedents: they support the methodological point that uvdin dḥol is not a vague modernity veto.
